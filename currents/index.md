# Currents

Not a directory. A reading of the institutional landscape of the right to food and the right to health as it looks from the physiological rights framing.

Three blocs. The first traces a precedent that already exists and needs to be extended. The second traces where the living scientific work on the specificity of physiological adequacy is being done. The third traces the silences: three literatures whose objects overlap and whose citations do not.

The threshold is deliberate. A figure or an institution has its own page only when there is an argument to make about it. Everyone else is named in the narrative below where they belong and not otherwise.

---

## I. The Vienna triptych: a precedent to extend

Between 2003 and 2026, a small group of clinical-nutrition physicians, one philosopher and one jurist assembled the only intergovernmental and inter-continental instrument in existence that names access to nutritional care as a human right. Four texts, one argument, thirteen years of drafting.

The European anchor is [Council of Europe Resolution ResAP(2003)3](/currents/council-of-europe-resap-2003-3/), adopted 12 November 2003 by the Committee of Ministers under the Partial Agreement in the Social and Public Health Field. First European intergovernmental instrument to state that *access to a safe and healthy variety of food is a fundamental human right*. Non-binding, applied to the hospital, no formal implementation review at any anniversary, and quietly de-mandated when the Committee of Experts on Nutrition was dissolved in 2007. The soft-law text on which harder arguments can lean.

The intellectual architecture is the work of [Diana Cárdenas](/currents/diana-cardenas/), MD from Universidad El Bosque, PhD in philosophy from Franche-Comté (2016) under Corine Pelluchon. She coordinated the 2019 Cartagena Declaration for FELANPE, the 2020 formation of the International Working Group for Patients' Right to Nutritional Care, the 2021 position paper in *Clinical Nutrition* that supplied the theoretical apparatus, the 2022 Vienna Declaration itself, and the 2026 ESPEN Practical Guideline on Ethical Aspects of Medical Nutrition Therapy in which the argument enters the guideline machinery for the first time. Her filiation with Corine Pelluchon's phenomenology of nourishment is the philosophical spine of the whole programme.

The institutional passport is [Christiane Druml](/currents/christiane-druml/), Austrian jurist trained under Felix Ermacora, Chair of the Bioethikkommission at the Federal Chancellery since 2007, Director of the Josephinum in Vienna, UNESCO Chair on Bioethics at MedUni Wien. Last author of the 2016 and 2026 ESPEN ethical guidelines. That the Vienna Declaration was signed in Vienna in 2022 and that its update in prescriptive form has its anchoring author in Vienna is the concentration in one city and around one institution of the ethics apparatus that a rights-based reading of clinical nutrition requires.

The consolidating text is the [Vienna Declaration on the Human Right to Nutritional Care](/currents/vienna-declaration-nutritional-care/), signed 5 September 2022 at the 44th ESPEN Congress by ASPEN, ESPEN, FELANPE, PENSA, EFAD, EPF and more than seventy national societies, endorsed at the inaugural session by DG SANTE and WHO Europe. Its central operative formulation: *access to nutritional care is a human right intrinsically linked to the right to food and the right to health*.

The limit of the triptych is stated inside its own scope clause: *patients with disease-related malnutrition, in all settings and conditions*. The Declaration is a document on the hospitalized undernourished patient. It does not descend to the specificity of individual micronutrient adequacy in ostensibly well-fed populations, to the failure of serum tests to detect tissue depletion, to the twenty-year latency between the science on the physiological requirement of magnesium, of folate, of vitamin D and its reflection in public guidance. That is the specific extension the physiological rights argument developed here proposes to make.

## II. The living scientific work on physiological adequacy

Bruce Ames died on 5 October 2024. His laboratory at the Children's Hospital Oakland Research Institute had already dissolved into a UCSF facility by 2020. No successor centre bears his name. The intellectual programme, the Triage Theory that argues insufficient micronutrient intake below the clinical deficiency threshold causes DNA damage and long-latency disease, continues at one institution more than any other.

[Emily Ho](/currents/emily-ho/), Endowed Director of the Linus Pauling Institute at Oregon State University since 2017 (third since Balz Frei), published with Carmen Wong and Laura Beaver in June 2025 in *Frontiers in Molecular Biosciences* the clearest extension of the Triage Theory to a specific micronutrient by a laboratory that carries the argument institutionally. Twenty years of her own work show that marginal zinc status, at intake levels above the RDA threshold for acute deficiency but below the level required for full genomic stability, produces measurable DNA damage. The Ames network still active around her: Tory Hagen at LPI (co-founder of Juvenon with Ames in 1999), Cecilia Giulivi at UC Davis (co-editor of the 2026 *Frontiers* Ames-legacy special issue), Joyce McCann (principal co-author of the triage-theory review series in *FASEB Journal* and *PNAS*, 2005-2018).

Two figures adjacent to that network operationalize the same argument at the level of a measurable phenotype. Michael Fenech, founder of the cytokinesis-block micronucleus assay now used worldwide as the standard measure of chromosomal damage in human cells, has argued for four decades that nutritional adequacy should be defined in terms of genome integrity, not only in terms of preventing acute deficiency. His 2024 *European Journal of Nutrition* paper documents that low serum magnesium and elevated homocysteine amplify DNA damage in circulating lymphocytes at values inside the clinically acceptable ranges. Reinhold Vieth, Emeritus at the University of Toronto, has argued for three decades in the pages of the *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition* and elsewhere that the North American Dietary Reference Intake for vitamin D was set on the basis of a statistical error, that the resulting recommended intake is roughly an order of magnitude below the physiological requirement, and that this discrepancy has real consequences at higher latitudes. Ho, Fenech and Vieth are the mainstream researchers whose arguments are technically correct, formally acknowledged and institutionally without consequence. Their trajectories are the documentary base of the twenty-year-latency argument this resource develops.

## III. Three literatures that do not meet

The instructive fact about the landscape in 2026 is not the strength of any one of its currents but the absence of citation across them.

The UN Special Procedures apparatus on the right to health and the right to food does not cite the clinical-nutrition-rights corpus. [Tlaleng Mofokeng](/currents/tlaleng-mofokeng/), Special Rapporteur on the right to health from 2020 to 2026, produced a full report on food, nutrition and the right to health (A/78/185, 2023) that names sixteen micronutrients, calls out ultra-processed food industries by trade tactics, and enumerates sixteen state recommendations, without citing Vienna, Cárdenas, Cartagena or the ESPEN 2021 position paper. Michael Fakhri, Special Rapporteur on the right to food, does not cite them either.

The second-circle legal scholarship on the right to health and the normative content of the right to adequate food does not cite the clinical-nutrition-rights corpus. Alicia Ely Yamin at Harvard's Petrie-Flom Center, whose 2025 *Suffering, Power and Courts* examines the political theory beneath judicial responses to structural health injustice, does not cite Vienna. Lawrence Gostin at Georgetown, Katharina Ó Cathaoir on food law in Copenhagen, do not cite Vienna. The clinical-nutrition rights argument and the international-human-rights-law argument have proceeded in parallel and have not met.

Symmetrically, the clinical-nutrition-rights corpus does not cite the biochemical apparatus that would give its argument physiological specificity. Neither the Vienna Declaration, the 2021 position paper, nor the 2026 ESPEN Practical Guideline cites the Triage Theory, Emily Ho's zinc work, Michael Fenech's genome-integrity argument, or Reinhold Vieth's vitamin D critique. The two arguments that share a subject, the human body's physiological entitlements, do not read each other.

Three literatures, three vocabularies, one silence at each of their intersections. That the physiological rights argument developed in this resource has to route between them is the specific task of the collection this page indexes.
