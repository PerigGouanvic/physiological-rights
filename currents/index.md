# Currents

Not a directory. A reading of the institutional landscape of the right to food and the right to health as it looks from the physiological rights framing.

Three blocs. The first traces a precedent that already exists and needs to be extended. The second traces where the living scientific work on the specificity of physiological adequacy is being done. The third traces the silences: three literatures whose objects overlap and whose citations do not.

The threshold is deliberate. A figure or an institution has its own page only when there is an argument to make about it. Everyone else is named in the narrative below where they belong and not otherwise.

---

## Working reading brief

The three blocs further down describe the landscape. This section reads a small number of the primary texts closely and derives from that reading the pivots on which a focused rayonnement can rest. It is placed in front of the blocs on purpose.

### A close reading of the Vienna Declaration

The [Vienna Declaration on the Human Right to Nutritional Care](https://www.espen.org/files/Vienna-Declaration-2022.pdf) is a six-page document with a preamble, three articles, and one appendix of signatures. The [DGEM annotated version](https://www.dgem.de/sites/default/files/PDFs/Vienna%20Declaration%20%20FINAL%205_2022.pdf) prints the same operative text over eight pages with numbered signatories. What follows reads the operative text with a sentence-level attention that a specialist reader would recognize.

The preamble is built on four *universally recognized* propositions, in this order: the right to health includes food and nutrition as socio-economic determinants; the right to food must be respected *in all spheres, including the clinical setting*; disease-related malnutrition has a specific description as a frequent condition caused *by virtually any disease*; and *the right to food is often overlooked in the clinical setting*. The four propositions frame nutritional care as the intersection where a general right (food) meets a specific clinical failure (disease-related malnutrition). They do not descend below that clinical framing. The word *micronutrient* appears only once in the whole document, and only inside the citation of the FAO-WHO Rome Declaration of 2014.

The preamble then chains eleven prior instruments. It matters that they appear in this order and no other. First the UDHR article 25 (1948), then ICESCR articles 11 and 12 with an explicit invocation of General Comment 14 by name (1966, 2000). Then Council of Europe ResAP(2003)3, cited *as ResAP(2003)2* by a copy-editing error the authors did not catch, on food and nutritional care in hospitals. Then UNESCO's Universal Declaration on Bioethics and Human Rights article 14 (2005) on progress in science advancing access to nutrition. Then Cancún (2008), Helsinki (1964/2013), Cordoba (2020), the Rome Declaration (ICN2, 2014), the SDGs (2015), the ESPEN 2016 ethical guideline on artificial nutrition and hydration, the ESPEN 2020 NEMS Manifesto on undergraduate medical curricula, the 2021 ASPEN position on artificially administered nutrition, and Cárdenas et al. 2021 as the international position paper on clinical nutrition and human rights. Reading the preamble backwards gives the direct genealogy: Cárdenas 2021, ESPEN 2016 and 2020, Cartagena 2019 (mentioned in the last bullet), Cancún 2008, ResAP 2003. Reading it forwards gives the international-law scaffolding the authors want to lean on: UDHR, ICESCR, UNESCO bioethics.

The typographical error that makes the Council of Europe resolution appear as ResAP(2003)2 rather than ResAP(2003)3 is a small observation, but it is exactly the kind of textual detail that an engaged reader can raise with the authors as evidence of having read at that level of attention. ResAP(2003)2 exists (it is on the *use of statistical methods in adjudicating claims arising out of medical negligence* and has nothing to do with nutrition). The correct reference is ResAP(2003)3.

Article 1 (Scope) is where the Declaration limits itself. Two sentences carry the load. The first is the operative formulation of the right: *access to nutritional care is a human right intrinsically linked to the right to food and the right to health*. The second is the scope clause that immediately narrows it: *in all patients with disease-related malnutrition, in all settings and conditions*. The Declaration is a hospital instrument. The words *disease-related* fix that scope. Anyone who wants to extend it to ostensibly well-fed populations, to the specificity of individual micronutrient adequacy, to the twenty-year latency between physiological science and public guidance, has to argue that extension explicitly and cannot assume that the Declaration already covers it. It does not.

Article 2 (Aim) declares four aims, each starting with a verb: *promote, provide, define, raise awareness*. The verbs are declaratory, not obligatory. No aim generates a state duty.

Article 3 (Principles) is where the operational content sits. It has five sub-sections numbered 3.1 through 3.5. Three of them repay close reading.

**Article 3.2.2** requires that all healthcare students receive mandatory instruction in *human nutrition in its three different domains, including basic nutrition, applied or public health nutrition, and clinical nutrition*. The tripartite curriculum formula is the single most operational commitment in the whole Declaration. Public health nutrition is one third of it. Any argument that the Vienna instrument authorizes a physiological-rights extension into the general population runs through 3.2.2.

**Article 3.3.5** reads: *The Human rights' FREDA principles (Fairness, Respect, Equality, Dignity, and Autonomy) are central to clinical nutrition practice.* The FREDA acronym is imported from UK mental health law (Curtice and Exworthy, *Psychiatric Bulletin* 2010). Its presence at this numbered position is Cárdenas's Pelluchon-trained hand: it substitutes a human-rights vocabulary for the Beauchamp-Childress principlist quartet (autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice) that appears in the preceding paragraph 3.3.4. The two paragraphs are printed next to each other on the page. Article 3.3.4 says *the internationally recognized principles of autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence and justice*. Article 3.3.5 says *the Human rights' FREDA principles*. The juxtaposition is deliberate. It is the smallest visible sign of the philosophical shift the authors are trying to make.

**Article 3.4.1** requires that *all nutritional care must include an after-hospital discharge plan, involving patients and caregivers, and be subject to an annual audit*. The annual audit clause has, to my knowledge, never been reported on since 2022. A reader in 2026 can ask whether any of the seventy-plus signatory societies has published one.

**Article 3.5.2** is one of the very few lines in the Declaration that reads as if written and not drafted: *Empowerment means education, and education is equal to freedom. Empowering patients is to offer them the opportunity to be part of the disease process and treatment.* The line is unusual in a document of this genre and was probably contributed by Correia (co-author, PENSA, Brazil), who publishes on patient empowerment in *Nutrition in Clinical Practice*.

What the Declaration does not say is worth naming. It does not name any nutrient. It does not name any specific test, threshold or reference intake. It does not name any of the researchers of the Triage Theory tradition. It does not cite the 2014 Rome Declaration's list of forms of malnutrition (undernutrition, micronutrient deficiencies, overweight and obesity) as a taxonomy it accepts; it cites Rome only in a preambular block quote. Its universe is disease-related malnutrition in the hospitalized patient. That is a large and important universe. It is not the universe of the physiological rights argument developed on this resource, and the gap between the two is not a hostility but a specific and productive editorial opportunity.

### A close reading of ResAP(2003)3 as delivered by the BAPEN Alliance

The full Council of Europe resolution is not open-access online. The [BAPEN Council of Europe Alliance 2-page leaflet](https://www.bapen.org.uk/pdfs/coe_leaflet.pdf) is the workable substitute. It preserves the operative content and adds an implementation apparatus.

The resolution has five broad areas: nutritional assessment and treatment in hospitals; nutritional care providers; food services practices; hospital food; health economics. It contains over a hundred numbered recommendations. The BAPEN Alliance summarized them as ten *Key Characteristics of good nutritional care in hospitals*. The first three are, in order: all patients screened on admission and re-screened weekly; all patients have a nutritional care plan; the hospital includes specific guidance on food services and nutritional care in its Clinical Governance arrangements. The sixth reads *All staff have the appropriate skills and competencies needed to ensure that patient's nutritional needs are met. All staff receive regular training on nutritional care and management.*

The BAPEN Alliance itself is instructive as a template. On the non-government side: the British Dietetic Association, Hospital Caterers Association, Royal College of Nursing, Royal College of Physicians, Royal College of Speech and Language Therapy, National Association of Care Catering, British Medical Association, BAPEN, Intercollegiate Group on Nutrition Education, Nutrition Society. On the government side: the National Patient Safety Agency, the Department of Health, Scottish Executive QIS, the Welsh Assembly Government, and the Department of Health and Public Service Northern Ireland. Twenty years later, no equivalent inter-professional alliance around Vienna 2022 exists in France, in Canada or in Belgium. Reading the BAPEN Alliance page as a template is one of the concrete organizational lessons this material yields.

The 2003 recommendations to member state governments were three: implement national recommendations on food and nutritional care in hospitals, promote implementation in public and private sectors, and ensure the widest possible dissemination. The natural twenty-year-audit question is: in the eighteen states that signed the Partial Agreement in the Social and Public Health Field in 2003, how many of the three recommendations have been demonstrably fulfilled? The answer is not obvious. The Committee of Experts on Nutrition was dissolved in 2007 and no formal review body exists.

### Candidates for a focused pivot

The rayonnement of a small-scale editorial project does not come from covering the whole landscape. It comes from a defensible, textually documented alignment with one or two figures or documents whose institutional weight is disproportionate to their public visibility. Seven such candidates exist in the material assembled here. They are listed with the case for and against making each of them the pivot.

**1. Diana Cárdenas, the intellectual architect.** MD Bogotá, PhD philosophy Franche-Comté 2016 under Corine Pelluchon, chief editor of *Revista de Nutrición Clínica y Metabolismo*, coordinator of the Cartagena Declaration 2019, the 2021 *Clinical Nutrition* position paper, the 2022 Vienna Declaration and the 2026 ESPEN Practical Guideline on Ethical Aspects. She holds the pen. For: living, actively producing, publishing in a journal she edits, formed in the philosophical tradition (Pelluchon, phenomenology of nourishment) that a physiological-rights argument can build on rather than translate away from. Against: her programme is explicitly scoped to disease-related malnutrition in clinical settings. Extending it to the ostensibly well-fed populations of interest here is a productive tension, not a hostile one, but it is a tension. Best point of entry: the [2021 *Clinical Nutrition* position paper](https://www.espen.org/files/other/HumanRightsPositionPaper_CN.pdf), sixteen authors, doi 10.1016/j.clnu.2021.02.039. Second entry: contact Cárdenas at *Revista de Nutrición Clínica y Metabolismo* with a submitted commentary that extends the 2021 framework to ambulatory populations.

**2. Christiane Druml, the institutional passport.** Austrian jurist trained under Felix Ermacora, Chair Bioethikkommission at the Federal Chancellery since 2007, Director Josephinum since 2015, UNESCO Chair on Bioethics at MedUni Wien since 2016, IBC UNESCO member 2008-2016, last author of ESPEN 2016 and 2026 ethical guidelines. For: any citizen intervention that reaches her has a clear route into UNESCO bioethics discourse and into the ESPEN guideline machinery. Against: her file is hospital ethics, informed consent, end-of-life and assisted-suicide law; nutritional adequacy in the general population is adjacent to her work, not central. Best point of entry: the *ESPEN Guideline on Ethical Aspects of Artificial Nutrition and Hydration* (Druml et al. 2016, *Clinical Nutrition* 35:545-556) as prior art, then read Vienna 2022 through her authorship signature and draft an inquiry addressed to her at christiane.druml@meduniwien.ac.at.

**3. The Vienna Declaration itself, the document to master.** Six pages of operative text, signed 5 September 2022 by ASPEN, ESPEN, FELANPE, PENSA, EFAD, EPF and more than seventy national societies. For: it is a quotable, endorseable, extendable document with international standing that names access to nutritional care as a human right. Against: article 1 fixes its scope to *patients with disease-related malnutrition, in all settings and conditions*. Pivoting on the Declaration means either accepting that scope and working inside it, or arguing for a public-nutrition extension that its own authors have deliberately not made. Best point of entry: memorize articles 3.2.2, 3.3.4, 3.3.5, 3.4.1 and 3.5.2 in the order given here. They are the five load-bearing sentences. Then write the missing annual audit that article 3.4.1 requires and that no signatory has produced.

**4. Emily Ho and the Linus Pauling Institute, the scientific anchor.** Endowed Director of LPI at Oregon State since 2017, third holder since Balz Frei. Twenty years of laboratory work on marginal zinc status producing DNA damage at intake levels above the RDA threshold. Coauthor with Ames in the last years. LPI is the only research institution in North America carrying the Triage Theory as an active programme after Ames's death on 5 October 2024. For: a scientific pivot around LPI grounds the physiological-rights argument in a laboratory, not in a philosophy. It answers the *what physiological requirement* question with a name, a citation, a funded research programme. Against: LPI does not speak human-rights language and has no institutional relationship with the Vienna corpus. The translation work is on this side. Best point of entry: the Wong, Beaver and Ho paper in *Frontiers in Molecular Biosciences* June 2025 as the current state of the argument; then the Ames-McCann triage series in *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition* 2009-2018 as the intellectual foundation.

**5. Council of Europe Resolution ResAP(2003)3, the unfinished European audit.** Adopted 12 November 2003 by the Committee of Ministers, applied to hospital nutrition across eighteen states, quietly de-mandated when the Committee of Experts on Nutrition was dissolved in 2007. Contains the sentence *access to a safe and healthy variety of food is a fundamental human right*. For: it is the first European intergovernmental text on the subject, its twentieth anniversary passed in 2023 without any implementation review, and the gap between what it committed to and what member states have actually done is a documented, journalistically workable subject. The BAPEN Alliance in the United Kingdom is the only alliance built around it that produced sustained implementation work; France and Canada have no equivalent. Against: soft law, non-justiciable, mandate transferred to EDQM and dropped. Reviving it is an editorial gesture. Best point of entry: the [BAPEN Alliance leaflet](https://www.bapen.org.uk/pdfs/coe_leaflet.pdf) and its ten Key Characteristics as an implementation template; then the ResAP itself for the operative text (to borrow); then contact BAPEN's current secretariat to ask whether any twenty-year review is in the works.

**6. The three-literatures-do-not-meet observation, the meta-positioning.** The most original angle in the material here is not any one figure but the documented mutual silence between UN Special Procedures on the right to health and food, second-circle legal scholarship on the right to health, and the clinical-nutrition-rights corpus. For: this positioning is publishable as a review or perspective in a nutrition or bioethics journal, positions Perig as a bridge-builder rather than a partisan of any camp, and does not require choosing between Cárdenas, Druml and Ho. Against: it requires reading all three literatures seriously enough to substantiate the silence, which is at least six months of focused reading. Best point of entry: the [Mofokeng 2023 report](https://documents.un.org/doc/undoc/gen/n23/206/34/pdf/n2320634.pdf) checked for absence of any Vienna citation, then Yamin's *Suffering, Power and Courts* 2025 checked for the same absence, then the position paper (Cárdenas et al. 2021) checked for absence of Ames, Fenech and Vieth. The evidence base for the three-literatures argument is the intersection of three absences, each verifiable in a single afternoon.

**7. Mofokeng, Fakhri and the UN Special Rapporteur route, the high-visibility juridical channel.** Tlaleng Mofokeng's mandate as SR on the right to health ended in July 2026. Michael Fakhri's mandate as SR on the right to food continues. For: an intervention that gets read at the level of Special Procedures reaches the ICESCR treaty body machinery and thence state reporting cycles. It is the shortest documented path from citizen editorial work to soft international law. Against: mandate cycles are slow, submissions are one item among hundreds, and both current mandate-holders have already produced reports on food and nutrition without citing the Vienna chain. Best point of entry: a submission to the OHCHR call for input on Fakhri's next thematic report, arguing that the omission of the clinical-nutrition-rights corpus in Mofokeng 2023 (A/78/185) is the specific evidentiary gap the mandate should close.

### Primary reading available online

Nine primary texts. Each URL below is public, free of paywall, and downloadable as PDF.

- [International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (1966)](https://treaties.un.org/doc/Treaties/1976/01/19760103%2009-57%20PM/Ch_IV_03.pdf). The treaty. Articles 11 (adequate food) and 12 (highest attainable standard of health). Full multilingual authentic text.
- [CESCR General Comment 12 on the right to adequate food, as printed inside OHCHR Fact Sheet 34 (1999)](https://www.ohchr.org/sites/default/files/Documents/Publications/FactSheet34en.pdf). The canonical UN legal interpretation of what *adequate food* means.
- [CESCR General Comment 14 on the right to the highest attainable standard of health (2000)](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/425041/files/E_C.12_2000_4-EN.pdf). The AAAQ framework (availability, accessibility, acceptability, quality) and the tripartite state duty (respect, protect, fulfil). Cited by name in the Vienna preamble.
- [FELANPE Declaración de Cancún (2008)](https://scielo.isciii.es/pdf/nh/v23n5/original1.pdf). Origin of the FELANPE chain. First formal appearance of *the human right of patients to receive opportune and optimal nutritional therapy*. In *Nutrición Hospitalaria* 23(5):413-417.
- [Cárdenas et al. 2021 international position paper in *Clinical Nutrition*](https://www.espen.org/files/other/HumanRightsPositionPaper_CN.pdf). Sixteen authors. Full theoretical apparatus of the human-right-to-nutritional-care programme: rights-based approach, tripartite duty, tables of normative content, duty-bearers and clinical implications. The Vienna Declaration is the declaratory translation of this paper. Start here for the theoretical spine.
- [Vienna Declaration, ESPEN 6-page version (2022)](https://www.espen.org/files/Vienna-Declaration-2022.pdf). The operative text.
- [Vienna Declaration, DGEM 8-page annotated version (2022)](https://www.dgem.de/sites/default/files/PDFs/Vienna%20Declaration%20%20FINAL%205_2022.pdf). Same text with numbered signatories, footnotes and fuller preamble. Use for verbatim citation.
- [BAPEN Alliance 2-page leaflet on Council of Europe ResAP(2003)3](https://www.bapen.org.uk/pdfs/coe_leaflet.pdf). The workable synthesis of the ResAP for readers who cannot get to the full 12-page official text. Contains the operative sentence and the ten Key Characteristics.
- [Mofokeng 2023 report on food, nutrition and the right to health (A/78/185)](https://documents.un.org/doc/undoc/gen/n23/206/34/pdf/n2320634.pdf). UN Special Rapporteur on the right to health. Names sixteen micronutrients, enumerates ultra-processed food industry tactics, sixteen state recommendations. Documents by its silence the absence of any UN citation of the Vienna chain.

### Notes on method for the coming weeks

Three considerations may help in narrowing the focus.

First, no pivot is exclusive. Choosing Cárdenas does not preclude reading Ho, and choosing the ResAP(2003)3 audit does not preclude engaging Druml. The question is which of the seven has enough textual weight to carry the first substantial editorial piece of this resource oriented outward toward a specialist reader.

Second, the most persuasive intervention a citizen can make in a specialist literature is not the corrective one but the extension one. The Vienna authors will read a text that takes their document seriously and shows what it does not yet cover. They will not read a text that reproaches them for a scope they explicitly chose. The FREDA paragraph 3.3.5 next to the Beauchamp-Childress paragraph 3.3.4 is a signal of where the authors themselves see the philosophical opening. A reader who catches that signal signals in return.

Third, the shortest visible task in the material is the annual audit article 3.4.1 requires and no signatory has produced. Writing a first-pass audit of Vienna 2022's implementation by its own signatory societies, four years in, is a project scaled to the resource of one person and a few afternoons of correspondence. It would demonstrate a level of engagement that no letter of introduction can substitute for.

The Voyage embedding pipeline is not required for any of the above. It matters later, at the point where the third bloc below (the three literatures that do not meet) has to be substantiated with concordance-level evidence. For a first pass of close reading, the URLs above are enough.

## I. The Vienna triptych: a precedent to extend

Between 2003 and 2026, a small group of clinical-nutrition physicians, one philosopher and one jurist assembled the only intergovernmental and inter-continental instrument in existence that names access to nutritional care as a human right. Four texts, one argument, thirteen years of drafting.

The European anchor is [Council of Europe Resolution ResAP(2003)3](/currents/council-of-europe-resap-2003-3/), adopted 12 November 2003 by the Committee of Ministers under the Partial Agreement in the Social and Public Health Field. First European intergovernmental instrument to state that *access to a safe and healthy variety of food is a fundamental human right*. Non-binding, applied to the hospital, no formal implementation review at any anniversary, and quietly de-mandated when the Committee of Experts on Nutrition was dissolved in 2007. The soft-law text on which harder arguments can lean.

The intellectual architecture is the work of [Diana Cárdenas](/currents/diana-cardenas/), MD from Universidad El Bosque, PhD in philosophy from Franche-Comté (2016) under Corine Pelluchon. She coordinated the 2019 Cartagena Declaration for FELANPE, the 2020 formation of the International Working Group for Patients' Right to Nutritional Care, the 2021 position paper in *Clinical Nutrition* that supplied the theoretical apparatus, the 2022 Vienna Declaration itself, and the 2026 ESPEN Practical Guideline on Ethical Aspects of Medical Nutrition Therapy in which the argument enters the guideline machinery for the first time. Her filiation with Corine Pelluchon's phenomenology of nourishment is the philosophical spine of the whole programme.

The institutional passport is [Christiane Druml](/currents/christiane-druml/), Austrian jurist trained under Felix Ermacora, Chair of the Bioethikkommission at the Federal Chancellery since 2007, Director of the Josephinum in Vienna, UNESCO Chair on Bioethics at MedUni Wien. Last author of the 2016 and 2026 ESPEN ethical guidelines. That the Vienna Declaration was signed in Vienna in 2022 and that its update in prescriptive form has its anchoring author in Vienna is the concentration in one city and around one institution of the ethics apparatus that a rights-based reading of clinical nutrition requires.

The consolidating text is the [Vienna Declaration on the Human Right to Nutritional Care](/currents/vienna-declaration-nutritional-care/), signed 5 September 2022 at the 44th ESPEN Congress by ASPEN, ESPEN, FELANPE, PENSA, EFAD, EPF and more than seventy national societies, endorsed at the inaugural session by DG SANTE and WHO Europe. Its central operative formulation: *access to nutritional care is a human right intrinsically linked to the right to food and the right to health*.

The limit of the triptych is stated inside its own scope clause: *patients with disease-related malnutrition, in all settings and conditions*. The Declaration is a document on the hospitalized undernourished patient. It does not descend to the specificity of individual micronutrient adequacy in ostensibly well-fed populations, to the failure of serum tests to detect tissue depletion, to the twenty-year latency between the science on the physiological requirement of magnesium, of folate, of vitamin D and its reflection in public guidance. That is the specific extension the physiological rights argument developed here proposes to make.

## II. The living scientific work on physiological adequacy

Bruce Ames died on 5 October 2024. His laboratory at the Children's Hospital Oakland Research Institute had already dissolved into a UCSF facility by 2020. No successor centre bears his name. The intellectual programme, the Triage Theory that argues insufficient micronutrient intake below the clinical deficiency threshold causes DNA damage and long-latency disease, continues at one institution more than any other.

[Emily Ho](/currents/emily-ho/), Endowed Director of the Linus Pauling Institute at Oregon State University since 2017 (third since Balz Frei), published with Carmen Wong and Laura Beaver in June 2025 in *Frontiers in Molecular Biosciences* the clearest extension of the Triage Theory to a specific micronutrient by a laboratory that carries the argument institutionally. Twenty years of her own work show that marginal zinc status, at intake levels above the RDA threshold for acute deficiency but below the level required for full genomic stability, produces measurable DNA damage. The Ames network still active around her: Tory Hagen at LPI (co-founder of Juvenon with Ames in 1999), Cecilia Giulivi at UC Davis (co-editor of the 2026 *Frontiers* Ames-legacy special issue), Joyce McCann (principal co-author of the triage-theory review series in *FASEB Journal* and *PNAS*, 2005-2018).

Two figures adjacent to that network operationalize the same argument at the level of a measurable phenotype. Michael Fenech, founder of the cytokinesis-block micronucleus assay now used worldwide as the standard measure of chromosomal damage in human cells, has argued for four decades that nutritional adequacy should be defined in terms of genome integrity, not only in terms of preventing acute deficiency. His 2024 *European Journal of Nutrition* paper documents that low serum magnesium and elevated homocysteine amplify DNA damage in circulating lymphocytes at values inside the clinically acceptable ranges. Reinhold Vieth, Emeritus at the University of Toronto, has argued for three decades in the pages of the *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition* and elsewhere that the North American Dietary Reference Intake for vitamin D was set on the basis of a statistical error, that the resulting recommended intake is roughly an order of magnitude below the physiological requirement, and that this discrepancy has real consequences at higher latitudes. Ho, Fenech and Vieth are the mainstream researchers whose arguments are technically correct, formally acknowledged and institutionally without consequence. Their trajectories are the documentary base of the twenty-year-latency argument this resource develops.

## III. Three literatures that do not meet

The instructive fact about the landscape in 2026 is not the strength of any one of its currents but the absence of citation across them.

The UN Special Procedures apparatus on the right to health and the right to food does not cite the clinical-nutrition-rights corpus. [Tlaleng Mofokeng](/currents/tlaleng-mofokeng/), Special Rapporteur on the right to health from 2020 to 2026, produced a full report on food, nutrition and the right to health (A/78/185, 2023) that names sixteen micronutrients, calls out ultra-processed food industries by trade tactics, and enumerates sixteen state recommendations, without citing Vienna, Cárdenas, Cartagena or the ESPEN 2021 position paper. Michael Fakhri, Special Rapporteur on the right to food, does not cite them either.

The second-circle legal scholarship on the right to health and the normative content of the right to adequate food does not cite the clinical-nutrition-rights corpus. Alicia Ely Yamin at Harvard's Petrie-Flom Center, whose 2025 *Suffering, Power and Courts* examines the political theory beneath judicial responses to structural health injustice, does not cite Vienna. Lawrence Gostin at Georgetown, Katharina Ó Cathaoir on food law in Copenhagen, do not cite Vienna. The clinical-nutrition rights argument and the international-human-rights-law argument have proceeded in parallel and have not met.

Symmetrically, the clinical-nutrition-rights corpus does not cite the biochemical apparatus that would give its argument physiological specificity. Neither the Vienna Declaration, the 2021 position paper, nor the 2026 ESPEN Practical Guideline cites the Triage Theory, Emily Ho's zinc work, Michael Fenech's genome-integrity argument, or Reinhold Vieth's vitamin D critique. The two arguments that share a subject, the human body's physiological entitlements, do not read each other.

Three literatures, three vocabularies, one silence at each of their intersections. That the physiological rights argument developed in this resource has to route between them is the specific task of the collection this page indexes.
