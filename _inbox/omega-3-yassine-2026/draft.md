# The Blunt Instrument Was the Study

On June 18, 2026, *eBioMedicine* published Yassine et al., "CNS target engagement of high-dose DHA supplementation in older adults at risk for dementia." That same day, CNN carried the story on its wellness desk and told readers what to do with their fish oil. The trial's principal investigator, Hussein Yassine of the University of Southern California, delivered the line the wire services wanted: *"Omega-3 supplements as a blunt instrument do not work."*

That sentence is now the piece of the study most readers will ever meet. It is also a self-portrait of the study itself. Two grams of pure DHA per day, for twenty-four months, in 365 adults whose low omega-3 status was confirmed by blood measurement (mean baseline index at 4.9%, well under the 8% threshold Harris and Von Schacky proposed twenty years ago), then randomized without any stratification by the depth of that deficit. A participant at 3% and a participant at 6% land in the same arm, and the signal of the truly depleted gets diluted by the merely low. One molecule at one dose in a pool that was correctly identified as deficient and then treated as homogeneous. That is the instrument. The trial tested DHA. It did not test EPA, it did not test the balance between EPA and arachidonic acid in synaptic membranes, it did not test the omega-3/omega-6 ratio that a half-century of British lipid biochemistry has identified as the operational variable. It tested one molecule at one dose, and it did what its title said it did: it engaged a target.

That title is worth reading twice. *CNS target engagement of high-dose DHA supplementation.* The phrase *target engagement* comes from early-phase drug discovery, where it names the question of whether a candidate molecule actually reaches the receptor it was designed to bind. Importing that vocabulary into a nutritional trial is not a stylistic choice, it is a conceptual displacement. It shifts the question from *how does a person live with a chronic deficit* to *did our molecule knock on the door*. Blood omega-3 index rose from 4.9% to 11%. In the 181 participants who consented to a lumbar puncture, the cerebrospinal DHA/arachidonic-acid ratio moved from 0.43 to about 0.60. The molecule got where it was sent. The paper is a pharmacokinetic success. Its authors then concluded, and CNN then relayed, that this success amounts to a failure of the nutrient. That step is not a scientific conclusion, it is a rhetorical maneuver, and it requires four unstated premises to hold: that DHA carries the biology, that the recipients were the right recipients, that the biomarker they moved was the biomarker that mattered, and that the mechanism the authors themselves flagged in their own discussion section is not important enough to disturb the verdict.

None of the four holds up. What follows takes them one at a time.
