# The Blunt Instrument Was the Study

On June 18, 2026, *eBioMedicine* published Yassine et al., "CNS target engagement of high-dose DHA supplementation in older adults at risk for dementia." That same day, CNN carried the story on its wellness desk and told readers what to do with their fish oil. The trial's principal investigator, Hussein Yassine of the University of Southern California, delivered the line the wire services wanted: *"Omega-3 supplements as a blunt instrument do not work."*

That sentence is now the piece of the study most readers will ever meet. It is also a self-portrait of the study itself. Two grams of pure DHA per day, for twenty-four months, in 365 adults selected on a single crude criterion (low dietary DHA), stratified only by APOE genotype, with no attempt to sort participants by the depth of their actual deficiency. One molecule at one dose in a heterogeneous pool. That is the instrument. The trial did not test omega-3 supplementation. It tested the version of it that could be executed inside the imaging pipelines and regulatory frames of a large American academic center. Whatever the biology of omega-3 in the aging brain turns out to be, this study was not built to see it.

The trial's own title says so, in Latin cladding. *CNS target engagement*: we succeeded in delivering the molecule to the tissue. Blood omega-3 index rose from 4.9% to 11%. In the 181 participants who consented to a lumbar puncture, the cerebrospinal DHA/arachidonic-acid ratio moved from 0.43 to about 0.60. The molecule got where it was sent. The paper is a pharmacokinetic success. Its authors then concluded, and CNN then relayed, that this success amounts to a failure of the nutrient. That step is not a scientific conclusion, it is a rhetorical maneuver, and it requires four unstated premises to hold: that DHA carries the biology, that the recipients were the right recipients, that the biomarker they moved was the biomarker that mattered, and that the mechanism the authors themselves flagged in their own discussion section is not important enough to disturb the verdict.

None of the four holds up. What follows takes them one at a time.
