# The Blunt Instrument Was the Study

On June 18, 2026, *eBioMedicine* published Yassine et al., "CNS target engagement of high-dose DHA supplementation in older adults at risk for dementia." That same day, CNN carried the story and told readers what to do with their fish oil. The trial's principal investigator, Hussein Yassine of the University of Southern California, delivered the line the wire services wanted: *"Omega-3 supplements as a blunt instrument do not work."*

That sentence is now the piece of the study most readers will ever meet. It is also a self-portrait of the study itself. Two grams of pure DHA per day, for twenty-four months, in 365 adults whose low omega-3 status was confirmed by blood measurement (mean baseline index at 4.9%, well under the 8% threshold Harris and Von Schacky proposed twenty years ago), then randomized without any stratification by the depth of that deficit. A participant at 3% and a participant at 6% land in the same arm, and the signal of the truly depleted gets diluted by the merely low. One molecule at one dose in a pool that was correctly identified as deficient and then treated as homogeneous. That is the instrument. The trial tested DHA. It did not test EPA. It did not manipulate the EPA-arachidonic-acid axis that a half-century of British lipid biochemistry has identified as the operational variable of neural signaling. It measured a shift in the cerebrospinal DHA/AA ratio, then treated that shift as evidence of biological engagement, a reading the paper's own discussion complicates and that section 3 will return to. The trial tested one molecule at one dose, and it did what its title said it did: it engaged a target.

That title is worth reading twice. *CNS target engagement of high-dose DHA supplementation.* The phrase *target engagement* comes from early-phase drug discovery, where it names the question of whether a candidate molecule actually reaches the receptor it was designed to bind. Importing that vocabulary into a nutritional trial is not a stylistic choice, it is a conceptual displacement. It shifts the question from *how does a person live with a chronic deficit* to *did our molecule knock on the door*. Blood omega-3 index rose from 4.9% to 11%. In the 181 participants who consented to a lumbar puncture, the cerebrospinal DHA/arachidonic-acid ratio moved from 0.43 to about 0.60. The molecule got where it was sent. The paper is a pharmacokinetic success. Its authors then concluded, and CNN then relayed, that this success amounts to a failure of the nutrient. That step is not a scientific conclusion, it is a rhetorical maneuver, and it requires four unstated premises to hold: that DHA carries the biology, that the recipients were the right recipients, that the biomarker they moved was the biomarker that mattered, and that the mechanism the authors themselves flagged in their own discussion section is not important enough to disturb the verdict.

None of the four holds up. What follows takes them one at a time.

## 1. The wrong molecule

The choice of pure DHA, at 2 g/day, was defensible on paper because of a story that lipid textbooks have been telling for decades: the omega-3 family is a metabolic continuum. ALA converts to EPA, EPA converts to DHA, DHA converts back to EPA when needed. The three molecules are functional equivalents linked by a reversible pathway. Give one, get the others. It is a tidy story, it is what most physicians who ever thought about omega-3 remember, and it is largely wrong.

Metherel and colleagues buried it in 2019, in the *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*, with a technique called compound-specific isotope analysis. Feed a subject stable-isotope-labeled DHA, then track the isotope's fate through EPA, DPA, and back. The finding was clean: the ALA → EPA → DHA arrow runs (poorly in men, better in women, always slowed by omega-6 competition). The DHA → EPA arrow does not run at all. What plasma EPA rises after DHA supplementation is not new EPA. It is old EPA whose catabolism has been slowed by the DHA flooding. The pool tilts without turnover.

When you give pure DHA for two years, the DHA pool fills. The EPA pool does not fill. The organism that had, at baseline, an omega-3 index of 4.9%, still has, at month 24, an EPA fraction determined by the little EPA it was already getting from the diet, plus whatever slowed catabolism has spared. The trial raised the D of DHA in that index. It did not raise the E of EPA.

EPA is not a spare version of DHA. It is the acid that regulates the release and signaling of arachidonic acid at the synapse. It is the acid that carries the antidepressant signal in the trials where it has been tested pure: Peet and Horrobin (2002), *Archives of General Psychiatry*, 53% response at 1 g/day of pure EPA against 29% on placebo, in patients whose depression had resisted standard care. It is the acid to which a half-century of British lipid biochemistry has assigned functional, minute-by-minute signaling, in contrast to the slow structural role assigned to DHA. A trial that raises DHA and leaves EPA where it was tests the structural molecule alone, in the absence of the signaling molecule that shares the pathway with it.

The Yassine team was not required to know this. Their school is amyloid neurology and APOE lipid transport, and their reviewers came from the same world. In a discipline where the word "supplement" is spoken with the same distance as the word "popping", a fifty-year British literature on EPA and cPLA2 does not have to be defeated. It simply does not enter the room. The choice of DHA-only was not a strategic exclusion of EPA. It was the exclusion of a literature that a neurology-APOE-amyloid school never treated as central. The choice was framed as neutral, on regulatory, pharmacokinetic, and dose-response grounds. It was not neutral. It excluded, in effect, the half of the omega-3 family whose functional role in nervous tissue is best documented.

## 2. The wrong recipients

The trial stratified its randomization by APOE ε4 status, which is defensible: APOE4 modifies lipid transport and increases dementia risk, and any DHA intervention that ignored it would have been criticized for confounding. What the trial did not stratify by is the axis its own inclusion criterion pointed at: the depth of the omega-3 deficit at baseline. A single blood measurement (mean baseline omega-3 index at 4.9%) established that the pool was collectively low. It did not sort within that pool. A participant whose index came in at 3.2% was randomized alongside one at 6.4%, and their responses were averaged together over 24 months.

The published subgroup data hint at what that averaging cost, and at something worse. Non-APOE4 carriers improved on cognition (+3.79 RBANS points against +1.60 in carriers), regardless of treatment. In the APOE4 subgroup, left hippocampal volume declined *more* on DHA than on placebo. In the population the trial was designed to protect first, those at highest genetic risk of dementia, receiving the intervention was, in direction, worse than receiving nothing. The difference did not reach significance. The paper does not present it as a signal. But the direction is unmistakable, and it is compatible with the mechanism section 3 will describe: a DHA-only supplementation, over 24 months, that competitively suppresses arachidonic acid at the compartments where AA supports neuroplasticity. If the direction is real, the trial did not simply fail to help. It began to hurt. That the finding was buried in a subgroup analysis, non-significant, and unmentioned in the interview to CNN is what non-stratification by the biological variable of interest produces: a signal that is present, is unwelcome, and is protected by statistical noise.

The distinction matters. APOE ε4 is a marker of who is at risk of dementia. The omega-3 index is a marker of who has the deficit the intervention is meant to fix. These are two different things, and a trial that treats them as interchangeable is running the classic error of population studies: it tests whether a mean response exists in a group defined by risk, when the biological question is whether a specific response exists in the subset defined by need.

If the truly depleted, at the 5th percentile of the study's baseline index, responded differently from the merely low at the 95th percentile, the trial cannot say so. The data exist. They were not analyzed that way. The pre-registered analysis plan set the stratification on genotype, and the analysis followed the plan. That is not a scientific failing, that is a design choice. But it is a design choice that guarantees a certain kind of null result: any true responders within the pool have their signal diluted by the non-responders they were pooled with, and the trial's own capacity to detect the response it was built to look for is throttled at the door.

## 3. The wrong biomarker

The paper's headline biochemical result is that the cerebrospinal DHA/AA ratio moved from 0.43 to about 0.60 in the DHA arm at six months, against a shift of −0.02 in the placebo arm. That is the number the authors chose as evidence that the intervention had reached the tissue. On its face, it is impressive. It is also, in direction, ambiguous.

A ratio can rise for two reasons. The numerator can climb, or the denominator can fall. In the case of DHA and arachidonic acid, both movements happen when DHA is given alone, and they happen through a different biochemistry than EPA would use. EPA (C20:5) shares the twenty-carbon backbone of arachidonic acid (C20:4) and displaces AA directly, at the same phospholipid positions and at the same metabolic enzymes AA normally uses (cyclooxygenase, lipoxygenase, phospholipase A2). This is the head-on competition the British lipid lineage places at the center of neural signaling. DHA (C22:6), with its longer chain, does not compete for those positions. It exerts its pressure on AA indirectly, through incorporation into distinct phospholipid classes that alter membrane packing and enzyme access, and through competitive inhibition of the cytochrome P450 pathway shown by Arnold and colleagues in 2010, in the *Journal of Biological Chemistry*. Human red-cell studies confirm that DHA supplementation, even given alone, lowers arachidonic acid in the erythrocyte membrane at twelve to twenty-four weeks. The effect is less pronounced in brain tissue than in the periphery. The ratio moves. The question is what has moved it, in which compartment, and through which of two distinct mechanisms.

Yassine's team did not report separate DHA and AA cerebrospinal values, only their ratio. From the ratio alone, the reader cannot tell whether the brain has been enriched in DHA, depleted in AA, or both. The three scenarios are indistinguishable at the level of the measurement chosen, and only one of them is compatible with the story the paper tells.

Arachidonic acid, in the brain, is not a nuisance. It is roughly 12% of cerebral lipid mass. Its free form, liberated on demand by cytosolic phospholipase A2, is a signaling molecule in its own right: it modulates ion channels, activates kinases, gates neurotransmitter release, and serves as the direct precursor of eicosanoids that regulate long-term potentiation and long-term depression, the two cellular mechanisms of memory. Its metabolic descendants include the endocannabinoids that carry the biological signature of motivation. That AA matters for cognition is not a theoretical claim. The infant formula industry, which has no ideological reason to prefer AA to DHA and every commercial reason to prefer the cheaper additive, added AA to formulas because adding DHA alone did not produce the cognitive gains the trials demanded. The best cognitive outcomes in that literature came from formulas that carried both. Squeezing AA, as a byproduct of flooding a competing molecule, is not neutral. Whether the Yassine trial actually squeezed it in the brain, or only in the blood, or not at all, cannot be determined from what was measured.

A biomarker of intervention success that cannot distinguish between the enrichment of the target and the depletion of its functional partner is not, strictly, a biomarker of success. It is a biomarker of displacement. The paper celebrates the displacement, and the discussion, as section 4 will show, contains an admission that the authors themselves suspected the mechanism by which their own metric might mislead them.

## 4. The mechanism the authors named but did not follow

Buried in the discussion of the Yassine paper is this sentence: *"Pathological activation of enzymes such as calcium-dependent phospholipase A2 (cPLA2) may actively alter DHA and AA fates after supplementation, catabolizing these polyunsaturated fatty acids. This process attenuates the intended biochemical intervention at the subcellular compartments (synaptic membranes) most critical for cognition."*

Read what it says. The authors are proposing that in the aging brains they were treating, the enzyme cPLA2 might be actively breaking down both DHA and AA at synaptic membranes as fast as, or faster than, the supplementation could replenish them. If that is true, then the cerebrospinal fluid measurement, which samples what is circulating in the ventricles, does not represent what is happening at the synapses. The molecule engaged the target on paper, and the target kept degrading it in place. The metric of success measured what escaped the enzyme, not what integrated into the membrane.

That is an extraordinary admission to make in the discussion of a paper whose title is *CNS target engagement*. It says: our biomarker of engagement may not reflect engagement. It also says something more consequential: that the biological system these authors were intervening on may have an intrinsic, disease-modifying degradation of the very lipids they were supplementing, at the very sites where those lipids need to work. If that is right, then the intervention as designed cannot succeed. Flooding a leaky vessel raises the level in the pipes and not in the vessel.

The mechanism has a genealogy Yassine does not cite. David Horrobin in the 1970s, Michael Crawford in 1972, and their successors (Stephen Cunnane and the small clinical school that has treated cPLA2 dysregulation and phospholipid trafficking at the synapse as the central variable in cognition) built a model that placed exactly this problem at the center of neural lipid biology, from early neurodevelopment through late-life decline. Their model did not produce the multicenter trial. Yassine's did. And Yassine's discussion, without recognizing that lineage or drawing the conclusion it draws, names the mechanism the British school has been placing at the center of neural biology for fifty years. That the two paths reached the same enzyme independently is possible. That the one with the biology has been ignored and the one with the funding has been amplified is not a coincidence.

The conclusion is that the trial cannot rule out the intervention. It has to rule out its own design first. If cPLA2 catabolism dominates at the synapse in aging APOE4 carriers, then a trial that measured cerebrospinal DHA at month 6 and cognition at month 24 was measuring the wrong thing at the right target on two different clocks. Eighteen months separate the two measurements. If the enzyme catabolism the authors named is active across those months, the biomarker they trusted at month 6 tells them nothing about the tissue state at month 24 when cognition was assessed. The right thing to measure would have been synaptic phospholipid turnover, and no imaging pipeline exists to do that at scale in living humans. The tools of the field, and therefore the questions the field can ask, do not reach the level at which the authors themselves suspect the answer lies.

## 5. The discourse the trial reactivates

Read the sentences the wire services chose. Yassine's line about the Mediterranean tells us that "people are not popping supplements. They're eating fatty fish, exercising, meeting with friends and living slowly with less stress." Isaacson's line at the closing tells us that omega-3s are "a drop in the ocean" for people who "eat an ultraprocessed food diet and are overweight with signs of chronic disease." The framing is old and it is not, primarily, about biology. It sets a moral hierarchy of the deserving and the undeserving. The Mediterranean fisherman is deserving. The Western pill-taker is not. The molecule cannot rescue what lifestyle already forfeited.

Look at what that framing requires the trial to demonstrate. Yassine's population was selected for low omega-3 status *and* for at least one dementia risk factor: obesity, sedentary lifestyle, high blood pressure, high cholesterol. That is the population the intervention was designed to test. The null result is then read back through Isaacson as evidence that the population was too unhealthy for the supplement to work. If the intervention only works in people who don't need it (Yassine's own second recommendation, in the same interview, is that healthy APOE4 carriers with low omega-3 should eat fatty fish twice a week and take "a high-quality omega-3 supplement"), then the null result is not a null result about the molecule. It is a null result about the discourse's own criterion of eligibility.

The word "popping" is where the disdain becomes audible. It is not a scientific term. It figures the supplement-taker as a compulsive, thoughtless consumer, a hand reaching without judgment into a pill bottle. Set against "eating fatty fish, meeting with friends, living slowly," it turns the question from *what does this molecule do* into *what kind of person takes a capsule*. The physiological question dissolves into a lifestyle sneer. That is not medicine speaking. It is the culture war on chronic disease, ventriloquized through a principal investigator.

Isaacson's "drop in the ocean" earns its own inspection. In the frame he supplies, the person is the ocean, the capsule is the drop, and the drop is lost. Reverse the picture. The deficit is what has accumulated over decades of low-omega-3 eating. The intervention is a fresh but small correction against that accumulation. In this picture, the null result at 24 months is not a verdict on the molecule. It is a comment on the timescale on which physiological deficits are made and the timescale on which trials are funded.

The label "gold standard of science" that CNN attaches to the trial completes the frame. Once a randomized controlled trial has spoken, the discussion is closed to the reader. The reader who has actually read Yassine's discussion, and who knows the trial tested one member of the omega-3 family at one dose in a pool that was not sorted by depth of deficit, with a biomarker of ambiguous direction and a mechanism the authors themselves said might defeat the design, is left holding a much smaller conclusion than the reader who accepted the headline. The gold standard, in this case, is a phrase doing rhetorical work that the trial's own methods do not underwrite.

None of this is unique to Yassine or to CNN. It is a discourse that greets every negative supplement trial with the same reflex: the molecule is a shortcut, the shortcut does not exist, the lesson is to live better. The lesson always converges on lifestyle. The lesson does not attach any political demand, because lifestyle is the private responsibility of the individual, and no institution can be called to account for the deficit the individual carries. A physiological rights framework, by contrast, asks whether the individual has access to what is missing. That question does not appear in the CNN piece. It cannot appear, because the frame does not admit it.

## What the trial actually shows

The Yassine trial is not the failure of omega-3. It is the failure of a specific choice: to supplement the aging brain with one lipid at a high dose, in the absence of its functional partner, for 24 months, against the operating hypothesis of the very literature the paper's own discussion accidentally reactivates. That was not a scoping problem. It was, most likely, a mistake in what to supplement. In the population most at risk, it may have been a mistake that pushed the brain in the wrong direction.

The right to an optimal omega-3 status, as this resource has set it out, does not rise or fall on this trial. It rests on the biology of membranes, on the omega-3/omega-6 imbalance of modern Western diets, on the testable Omega-3 Index, and on a century of lipid biochemistry that identifies these acids as functionally required for the working brain. Yassine et al. did not test any of that. They tested a pharmacological intervention of the wrong composition, at a fixed dose, in a pool sorted by genotype rather than by depletion. It failed on its own terms. In the subgroup it was meant to help most, the direction of the hippocampal signal suggests it may have done worse than nothing.

The right stands. The next trial worth running is one this trial cannot design, and one this discourse would not know how to report.
