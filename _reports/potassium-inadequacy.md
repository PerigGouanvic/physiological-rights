# Case Report: Potassium Inadequacy

**Context**: An individual presents with symptoms compatible with chronic potassium insufficiency: palpitations, blood pressure at the border of hypertension, muscle weakness after exertion, occasional cramps. Diet review confirms a habitually low intake of unprocessed plant foods, legumes, and tubers — the main dietary sources of potassium. A blood test shows serum potassium within the reference range.

**Problem**: Because serum potassium is defended within a narrow band by renal and endocrine mechanisms, a normal lab value can coexist with a chronic inadequate intake for years. The physician, seeing a "normal K", finds no reason to intervene and issues generic advice about eating fruits and vegetables. Symptoms persist and blood pressure creeps upward.

**Physiological rights analysis**: The case exposes the gap between *hypokalemia* — a lab abnormality — and *dietary inadequacy* — an intake below the Adequate Intake. USDA/NHANES data show that median daily potassium intake in the US population aged 2+ has stood around 2500 mg for decades, well below the AI of 3400 mg (males) and 2600 mg (females) set by the National Academies. Roughly one half to three quarters of North American adults do not reach the AI on any given day. This is not an individual failing but a population-level state of chronic inadequacy, driven by a food system that has shifted intake away from unprocessed plant foods toward sodium-rich processed products. Physiological rights hold that when a nutrient with quantifiable cardiovascular benefit is under-consumed by the majority of the population, standard care that waits for laboratory hypokalemia before acting fails its duty of protection.

**Consequences**: Randomised controlled trials establish that increasing potassium intake lowers blood pressure in a dose-dependent manner. Meta-analyses of cohort studies link a higher sodium-to-potassium ratio linearly to stroke incidence and cardiovascular mortality. The invisibility of the deficit at the individual level — normal serum K, generic dietary advice, no follow-up — allows preventable disease to accumulate across decades and across entire populations. Beyond the cardiovascular endpoints, chronic potassium inadequacy is associated with kidney stone formation and accelerated bone mineral loss.

**Proposed solution**: Physiological rights argue for a shift from lab-triggered to intake-triggered intervention. Adequate potassium intake should be an operational target of both clinical care and public policy: dietary counselling backed by measured food-source data (the top contributors are fruits, vegetables, tubers, legumes, and dairy — not "eat healthier" in the abstract); structural reformulation of processed foods to lower the sodium-to-potassium ratio, on the model of iodisation programmes; and supplementation with medical supervision where clinically warranted. Waiting for hypokalemia to appear before acting misses the point — the physiological right is to the *adequate intake*, not merely to the avoidance of overt deficiency.
