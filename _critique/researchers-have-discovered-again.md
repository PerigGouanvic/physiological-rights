# Researchers Have Discovered, Again

*There is a genre of journalism about foods and nutrients that improve our health. It has rules, and it follows them so faithfully that the article can be written before the study is published. Here it is, with the blanks left open and every rule annotated.*

---

## The template

> **New Study Finds [NUTRIENT] May [LOWER / BOOST] Your Risk of [DISEASE]**
>
> A [surprising / landmark / groundbreaking] study from researchers at [UNIVERSITY] suggests that [NUTRIENT], found in [FOOD], could play a key role in [BENEFIT]. **①**
>
> "We were struck by the strength of the association," said Dr. [NAME], [TITLE] in the Department of [DEPARTMENT] at [UNIVERSITY] and lead author of the study, published in [JOURNAL]. **②**
>
> The team followed [N] participants for [YEARS] years and found that those with the highest intake of [NUTRIENT] had a [X] percent lower risk of [DISEASE] than those with the lowest. **③**
>
> Good sources of [NUTRIENT] include [FOOD 1], [FOOD 2], [FOOD 3] and [FOOD 4]. **④**
>
> But experts caution against rushing to the supplement aisle. "Most people can get all the [NUTRIENT] they need from a balanced diet," said [DIETITIAN], who was not involved in the study. Anyone considering supplements should talk to their doctor first. **⑤**
>
> The researchers stress that the study shows an association, not causation, and that more research is needed to confirm the findings. **⑥**

Swap magnesium for omega-3, choline for vitamin D, dementia for heart failure. The article survives every substitution. That is the first thing to notice: a text that works for any nutrient and any disease is not reporting on either.

---

## The annotations

### ① The perpetual first time

Every brick is presented as the foundation. The fortieth cohort linking low magnesium to cardiac arrhythmia is announced with the same surprise as the first, because the genre has no memory. It cannot have one. A memory would show the reader a building, and a building raises an awkward question: if the walls have been going up for sixty years, why are we still told to wait for the roof?

Amnesia here is structural. Each article resets the count to zero, and a count that always starts at zero can never reach a conclusion.

### ② The press office byline

The name, the title, the department, the university, the journal. This is the press release surviving into print, often nearly verbatim. University press offices write these releases to promote the institution, and the exaggerations they introduce travel into the news coverage that follows (Sumner and colleagues documented the pattern in the *BMJ* in 2014). The reader gets an affiliation where a context should be. Who funded the study, what earlier work it confirms, how long the field has known this: none of it fits in the slot.

### ③ The quintile ritual

Highest intake versus lowest intake, a relative risk, a percentage. The number is real and nearly useless to the person reading. Intake is not status. Two people eating the same plate of spinach can end up with very different tissue magnesium, depending on what they absorb, what they lose through the kidneys, what drugs they take, what stress and illness consume. The quintile tells you about a population sorted by its food diaries. It tells you nothing about which quintile your cells belong to.

### ④ The grocery list

Here the article quietly decides what kind of problem this is: a shopping problem. If you lack the nutrient, you ate badly; buy almonds. Losses, malabsorption, cofactors, increased demand in disease, medications that drain the very thing the article is praising, all of it falls outside the frame. The list is the genre's theory of physiology, and that theory is a menu.

### ⑤ The closed door

Two moves in one paragraph. First, a dietitian not involved in the study reassures you that food is enough, which contradicts the premise of the story you have just read (if food were enough, who are the people in the lowest quintile?). Then you are sent to your doctor.

Follow the instruction. The doctor will order a serum level if anything at all, and for magnesium, potassium or zinc that number is held steady by the body at the expense of the tissues, so it reads normal until the deficit is deep. A normal result, and the advice to eat a balanced diet. You are back at slot ④. The article has sent you around a loop and called it caution.

### ⑥ The deferral clause

"More research is needed." Always true, therefore empty. Who will do it? Nutrient research has no patent behind it and no sponsor to pay for the trials the clause demands. The sentence presents an absence of funding as scientific modesty, and it guarantees that the next article, written in five years from the next study, will end the same way. The clause closes the story while keeping the case open indefinitely.

---

## The missing slot

One paragraph never appears in the template, in any of its thousands of instances:

> *Here is how to find out whether you are among the people who lack it, and here is what to do if you are.*

That is the only paragraph a reader could act on, and the genre cannot print it. A real answer would require a test that measures tissue status rather than serum, a dose range that reflects therapeutic use rather than the dietary floor, and permission to say that some people need more than food will give them. Each of those breaks a rule above. So the article supplies everything except the two things you came for: whether you are deficient, and whether this molecule, at what dose, will do for you what the study suggests.

---

## The graduation that never comes

A body of evidence is supposed to mature. At some point the accumulated cohorts, mechanisms and trials should earn a change of verdict: from *eat well* to *check, and correct*. The template has no mechanism for that passage. It can absorb any quantity of findings and still produce the same last line, because its last line was never a conclusion drawn from the evidence. It was a fixed part of the format.

This is what makes the genre so exhausting to read over the years. It does not lie. Each article is broadly accurate about its study. The cumulative effect is a public that has been told about magnesium, vitamin D, omega-3 and choline hundreds of times, knows the food lists by heart, and still cannot learn whether its own body is short of any of them. Information without knowledge, reassurance without recourse.

The reader is addressed as a consumer of good news about vegetables. This resource addresses the same reader as the holder of a claim: to know what the body lacks, and to receive it. The template has no slot for that person.
