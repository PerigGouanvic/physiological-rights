# Practicing the Physiological Right in an Ordinary Life

*For the reader who has agreed with the arguments and still forgets. A right exercised imperfectly, and taken up again, is already the right exercised.*

## The gap this piece exists for

The two founding pieces of this resource on the ground of the physiological right, [*Care of Self as Ground of the Physiological Right*](/definitions/care-of-self-as-ground/) and [*The Fall of Hygiene*](/critique/the-fall-of-hygiene/), have done what they were built to do. They have retired the reasons we used to give ourselves for letting the practice slip. They have shown that the right does not depend on an affect that comes and goes, on a self-love unevenly distributed, on a vitality we may or may not feel today. They have installed the practice in a tradition twenty-five centuries old, and they have shown that the shame of not sustaining it belongs to a missing collective infrastructure rather than to the person who forgets.

Reading them, one might expect to walk out of the reading and, from the next morning on, keep the practice cleanly. That is not what happens. The reader forgets anyway. The author of these lines forgets. The forgetting is not a failure of understanding, it is what an ordinary life does to a good intention.

This piece is for that gap. It is for the reader who has agreed and still forgets, and it refuses to close the gap by turning the reader into a project.

## What we are not

The social media feeds of the last decade have produced a figure who occupies, in the domain of the body, the position that the airbrushed beauties of an earlier era occupied in the domain of appearance. The wellness influencer, the biohacker, the morning-routine ambassador, the supplement stack curator, the ice-bath posting entrepreneur. Their conditions of production are not the conditions of a life. Unlimited time, sponsored gear, invisible medical support, camera-ready sleep, and the enormous economic incentive of an audience. Their function, whether they intend it or not, is the same as that of the glossy magazine ideals of an earlier decade. They make the ordinary reader feel inadequate before the ordinary reader has taken the first step. They monetise the very shame they produce.

The physiological right is not this. It has nothing to do with an optimised body posted for approval. It is what a citizen is owed for the ordinary functioning of an ordinary life. Its exercise looks nothing like a feed. It looks like a person who takes what their tissues need, on the days they remember, and who takes it up again on the days they had forgotten.

If the wellness performance is what a right rejected looks like when it is repackaged as a competition, the ordinary practice below is what the right actually looks like when it is exercised.

## Three levers for the ordinary life

Three practical leverages align with the ground the founding pieces installed. Each one acts on a specific mechanism that makes forgetting the default.

### Write down the before

Keep, somewhere, a single dated sentence describing your state before the intervention that worked. *Before potassium, palpitations at rest by evening and a broken sleep after three in the morning.* A phrase, not a journal. Nothing ambitious. Its purpose is to hold the reference the body will erase. Once the intervention has worked and the tissues have settled at the new baseline, the earlier state becomes literally unavailable to memory. The dated sentence restores, when you re-read it three months on, the comparison the body no longer performs. This lever acts against the quiet deletion of the difference the intervention made.

### Reframe the act

Move from *I take X because I lack it* to *I maintain a state I have obtained*. The first framing depends on a lack you must feel in order to be motivated. The second framing rests on who you are now, which is available to you without any signal. The reframing sounds cosmetic. It is not. It changes what schema the mind applies to the act. The lack-and-supply schema is the schema of curative medicine, which teaches that a problem resolved is a treatment ended. The maintenance schema is the schema of a right exercised, which continues in the absence of any acute reason to continue. It is also, incidentally, the truer description of what a body actually needs.

### Let one other person know

The rituals that survive across a lifetime, brushing teeth, morning coffee, meals at set times, are the rituals a culture holds in common. Supplementation, protein intake, resistance training, are practices that ordinary life has not yet ritualised. Their maintenance rests almost entirely on the individual, which is why they slip. One partial remedy is to let one other person know. A partner, a friend, a monthly conversation, a shared note. The mere presence of a witness, even a distant one, converts a solitary act into a practice, and a practice into a habit. Sociality does what will alone cannot.

## What to do when we fail

We will fail. Weeks will pass in which the routine dissolves. Trips, illness, grief, work, ordinary distraction, will interrupt the practice, and there is no shame in it. The founding pieces have retired the reasons for shame. What matters is not the unbroken record. What matters is the taking up again.

The antique tradition of the care of self had a word for the mental posture that made this taking up again possible: *prosochē*, attention. Not attention as strain, attention as return. The Stoics knew that the mind wanders and that the practice consists of noticing that it has wandered and coming back. The physiological right has the same shape. It is not held by the perfect run. It is held by the return.

## The formula

The physiological right is exercised by the person who takes what their tissues need on the days they remember, notes what worked before, mentions it to one other person, reframes the act as the maintenance of an obtained state, and takes it up again on the days they had forgotten. That practice is enough. It is the exercise of a right whose full collective infrastructure we do not yet have.

The reader who forgets is not a bad practitioner. They are a citizen exercising, imperfectly, a right whose supports remain to be built. The imperfect, ordinary, repeatedly resumed practice is the practice.
