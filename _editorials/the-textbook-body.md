# The Textbook Body

*Most people believe they live in the first chapter of the biology textbook, with a small appendix for their asthma or their few extra pounds. The arithmetic of the population says otherwise. The patient with a single deficiency, around whom trials and protocols are built, is about one adult in five thousand. The ordinary adult carries five.*

---

Ask someone how their health is and you will usually hear a version of the same sentence. *I'm fine. Well, apart from my asthma.* Apart from the extra twenty pounds, the blood pressure that crept up, the sleep that has not been good for years, the knee, the afternoons. Fine, with an appendix.

That sentence has a shape, and the shape comes from a book. Every biology and physiology textbook is built the same way. The first and longest part describes the body as it works: the cell with its membranes intact, the mitochondrion turning its cycle, the enzyme sitting on its cofactor, the neuron releasing exactly the transmitter it was built to release. Every pathway is fed. Every substrate arrives on time. Then, at the back, come the chapters on pathology, one disease at a time, each a localised failure in an otherwise flawless machine.

People believe they live in the first part of the book. They grant themselves one or two chapters from the back, for realism.

They do not live in that book. Nobody does.

---

## The body in the first chapter is a hypothesis

Look at what the textbook body requires to work as drawn. The Krebs cycle needs iron-sulfur clusters, thiamine, riboflavin, niacin, pantothenic acid, lipoic acid, magnesium. The membrane that holds a neuron's charge needs choline for its phospholipids and omega-3 fatty acids for its fluidity. The pump that keeps potassium inside the cell and sodium outside it, and that consumes a quarter of your resting energy doing so, needs magnesium to run and potassium to move. Every arrow on every diagram is a cofactor somebody had to eat.

The diagram does not say so. It shows the arrow, and the arrow is always drawn as if it were paid for. That is the first chapter's quiet assumption, and it is the assumption the reader carries out of the classroom, into the clinic, into the conversation about how they are doing: that the machinery is stocked by default, and that whatever goes wrong goes wrong *on top of* a complete body.

The body that the textbook describes is not a description. It is a hypothesis about a person who has been fed on every front at once. The interesting question, then, is how many such people exist.

---

## The arithmetic

This resource documents the population figures for a handful of nutrients, each on its own page. Take seven of them, the ones where the numbers are best established, and take the shortfall rates at face value:

- **Magnesium**: about half of Americans below the estimated requirement.{% include cite.html id="ods-magnesium" ex="intake" %}
- **Potassium**: well over nine in ten below the 4 700 mg that the physiology supports.{% include cite.html id="whelton-2018" ex="intakes" %}
- **Vitamin D**: about two in three below 75 nmol/L, taken here at the conservative end; three in four in the national survey that reported this threshold, before assays were standardised.{% include cite.html id="ginde-2009" ex="abstract" %}{% include cite.html id="herrick-2019" ex="abstract" %}
- **Omega-3**: nearly all, 97.6 % of American adults, below an Omega-3 Index of 8 % in the first national measurement (NHANES 2021–2023).{% include cite.html id="powers-2026" ex="results,abstract" %}
- **Choline**: about nine in ten below the Adequate Intake.{% include cite.html id="wallace-fulgoni-2016" ex="abstract" %}
- **Vitamin E**: roughly eight to nine in ten below requirement from diet alone.{% include cite.html id="reider-2020" ex="abstract" %}{% include cite.html id="traber-2014" ex="abstract" %}
- **Functional B12**: about one in ten, more with age, metformin, or acid suppression.{% include cite.html id="mineva-2021" ex="abstract" %}

The textbook body, the person adequate on all seven, is about one adult in several hundred thousand. The person with exactly one shortfall is about one in five thousand. The middle of the distribution, the ordinary adult, the person who tells you they are fine, carries **five**.

Add iron for a menstruating woman (ferritin under 30 μg/L in about a third of them) and the peak moves further right: one woman in ten is short on seven or eight of the eight. Add zinc, iodine, selenium, vitamin K2, taurine, glycine, and the curve keeps walking right. It never walks back.

## The patient who does not exist

Now look at the column for one.

That column is where medicine lives. The clinical trial that tests magnesium gives magnesium, and only magnesium, to people who are assumed to be otherwise replete, and measures whether something improves. The protocol that treats iron deficiency treats iron. The textbook chapter on B12 describes the B12-deficient patient as a textbook body with one cofactor removed. Every one of these designs presupposes a person with a single gap in an otherwise complete body.

That person is one adult in five thousand. The trials are run on the other 4 999.

When the magnesium trial on people who are also short on potassium, vitamin D, omega-3 and choline comes back with a modest or null effect, the conclusion printed in the abstract is that magnesium does little. The conclusion the arithmetic supports is different: one missing letter was handed back to people missing four others, and the word still could not be spelled. (On why a single restored nutrient so often cannot show its effect, see [One Missing Letter](/critique/one-missing-letter/) and [Calibrated for Nothing](/critique/calibrated-for-nothing/).)

A science organised around a patient who does not exist will, with great rigour and at great expense, keep finding that nutrients do not matter very much.

---

## The shortfalls travel together

The calculation above assumed independence. Reality is worse, and in an instructive direction.

Shortfalls are not dealt at random. The same refined grain strips magnesium, B vitamins and vitamin E at once. The same absence of fish removes omega-3 and selenium and iodine. The same proton-pump inhibitor lowers B12, magnesium, iron and zinc together. The same obesity sequesters vitamin D and comes with documented shortfalls in thiamine, folate, B12, iron and magnesium, before any surgery. The same poverty, the same institution, the same night shift, the same diuretic.

So the deficiencies concentrate. A small minority (well fed, sunlit, unmedicated, attentive, often supplemented) is adequate on more fronts than the independent model predicts. Everyone else carries more than it predicts, piled onto the same bodies. The ordinary adult carries five. The adult your health system worries about least, because they have no diagnosis yet, may well carry eight. (The arithmetic is worked through in [You Are Not the Exception](/editorials/you-are-not-the-exception/); the full portrait of the composite in [The Life We Call Normal](/editorials/the-life-we-call-normal/).)

## What the real body does instead

Here is the part the textbook never tells you, and it is the most remarkable thing about you.

The body short on five things does not stop. It triages. When magnesium runs low, it is pulled out of bone to keep the heart rhythm and the nerve membrane going, and the bone pays later. When cysteine is scarce, it is sent to glutathione for today's defence at the cost of tomorrow's protein. When vitamin K is short, the clotting factors are served first and the proteins that keep calcium out of the arteries and in the bone are left unactivated. The late Bruce Ames called this triage: short-term survival purchased with long-term repair, an exchange the organism makes silently, every day, for decades.

That is why the person carrying five shortfalls feels *fine*. The body is a genius at hiding the bill. It keeps every number on the routine panel in range by spending reserves that no routine panel measures. What you call your health is, quite often, the body's competence at concealing its debts.

And when the debts come due, they come due one at a time, each with a name from the back of the book. Hypertension. Insulin resistance. Osteoporosis. Depression. Atrial fibrillation. Early cognitive decline. Each chapter, in its turn, treated as a localised failure in an otherwise flawless machine.

---

## Why nobody told you

Because nobody looked, and because the instruments for looking were set not to see.

The tests that would show the composite are rarely ordered: not red-cell magnesium, not the Omega-3 Index, not choline, not tissue potassium, not methylmalonic acid ([The Workup They Do Not Order](/critique/the-workup-they-do-not-order/)). The ones that are ordered are read against reference ranges drawn from the very population that carries the shortfalls, so the shortfall becomes the norm by construction ([Ferritin 24](/critique/ferritin-24-a-model-case/), [The False Negative of Serum Potassium](/critique/false-negative-serum-potassium/)). And each specialty owns one chapter at the back of the book. The cardiologist reads the potassium; nobody reads the person.

The result is a population that has been told, accurately by the standards applied, that it is fine. The standards are the textbook body. The population is the histogram.

## What you are owed

This resource argues that the right to food, already written into international law, has a clinical content that has never been specified: the physiological parameters a body actually needs, the tests that would detect their absence, and thresholds set to detect it rather than to hide it. That specification is what we call [physiological rights](/definitions/physiological-rights/).

Seen from the histogram, the right is not exotic. It is the demand to be measured as the body you are, not as the body in the first chapter. It is the demand that the composite be assessed as a composite, and corrected as one. And it is the refusal of a science that keeps testing one letter at a time on people missing five, then publishing the silence as a finding.

The individual rights are set out one by one in [Rights](/rights/): [magnesium](/rights/magnesium/), [potassium](/rights/potassium/), [omega-3](/rights/omega-3/), [choline](/rights/choline/), [vitamin E](/rights/vitamin-e/), [iron](/rights/iron/), and more. If you want to know where to go next, whether you come as a jurist, a clinician or someone looking after yourself, start [here](/where-to-start/).

### A note on the numbers

The seven prevalence figures mix two kinds of data: intake surveys (magnesium, potassium, choline, vitamin E, compared with dietary reference values) and biomarkers (vitamin D, Omega-3 Index, functional B12). Intake below a reference value is not the same as tissue depletion, and each figure is contested on its own page, in both directions. The histogram assumes independence, which the section above explains is false in a way that widens both tails. None of this is meant as a census. It is the order of magnitude, and the order of magnitude does not move: under any defensible set of figures, the adult with zero or one shortfall is a statistical rarity, and the adult with several is the norm.
