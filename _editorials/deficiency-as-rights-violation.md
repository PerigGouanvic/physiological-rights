# Deficiency as a Rights Violation

Most people carry a set of deficiencies, in tissue magnesium, available iron, tissue potassium and others, each person in a combination of their own (see [You Are Not the Exception](/editorials/you-are-not-the-exception/)), and in most cases the standard of care neither tests for them nor corrects them. It is tempting to treat this as a shortcoming of medicine, to be fixed with better guidelines, one nutrient at a time. It is that, but it is first a violation of the right to health, and fixing it piecemeal would leave untouched the reason it happens, which is that medicine was built to recognise diseases and symptoms and has made it an imperative to bring these parameters back into range only for the few it measures routinely, at thresholds set to find disease.

When they see their doctor, most people assume they will be tested and that whatever is wrong will be corrected. The guidance points the other way, and in many countries at once. In the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada and Australia, the bodies that issue clinical guidance discourage measuring vitamin D in people without symptoms or risk factors, and Choosing Wisely, a campaign of medical societies now active in more than twenty countries, has made the reduction of such tests one of its causes. In Canada, for instance, the family physicians' list advises against ordering screening blood tests every year unless the patient's risk profile requires it, and it justifies not measuring vitamin D by the fact that supplementation is appropriate for the general population, as if no one needed to be measured because almost everyone is short. The measurement is replaced by a piece of advice, and the advice, when it is given at all, is left to the patient, so that fewer than a third of Canadians take any supplement containing vitamin D. "The blood test with twenty boxes ticked" is useless in people without symptoms, says Alain Vadeboncoeur, an emergency physician who has argued publicly against the routine checkup.[^translation] Within his frame he is right, since a panel read against thresholds set to find disease finds little disease in people who have none. Iron is the exception that shows the frame. Ferritin is often ordered when a patient is tired, it reflects stores rather than circulating levels, and it is read against a threshold, too low but a threshold (see [The Ferritin Threshold](/editorials/the-ferritin-threshold/)), so iron gets corrected. The other deficiencies meet none of these conditions.

The failure to correct is not, in most cases, a refusal in the ordinary sense. It is architectural. The routine test does not name the deficit when it is present (see [Calibrated for Nothing](/critique/calibrated-for-nothing/) on the construction of the reference ranges, and [The False Negative of Serum Potassium](/critique/false-negative-serum-potassium/) for the tissue-versus-serum problem in its clearest form). The deficit therefore never enters the differential, and the correction never enters the encounter. The clinician follows the standard of care in good faith, and it is the standard of care itself that is calibrated to miss what the right to health requires it to catch.

## "The right to health is not to be understood as a right to be healthy"

The United Nations committee that interprets the right to health in the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights wrote this sentence in 2000, in General Comment 14, its reference reading of that right. The Committee meant it as a limit, since no State can guarantee health against genetics, individual susceptibility or risky ways of living (paragraph 9), and so the right becomes a right to "the enjoyment of a variety of facilities, goods, services and conditions", which is how medicine works, offering services without answering for the state of the body. Within that limit, the deficit the routine test misses violates nothing, since the service was offered.

## Where health can be measured, it is a right to be healthy

The limit holds for what cannot be guaranteed. It does not hold for a measurable parameter that an inexpensive substrate brings back into range. The right to health is one of progressive realisation, bounded by the resources available to the State (Article 2 of the Covenant), but the same General Comment sets, at paragraph 43, **core obligations** from which a State cannot exempt itself even under resource constraint, among them access to essential drugs as defined by the WHO Model List and to "minimum essential food which is nutritionally adequate and safe", and the correction of a documented deficit, with a substrate that is cheap and available, sits inside this floor. Civil-law doctrine distinguishes an obligation of **means** (do your best with what the standard of care provides) from an obligation of **results** (the essential parameter shall be maintained), and for such a parameter the right to health requires the obligation of results.

## The State owes the result

The Covenant binds States, not clinicians, and the physician is held to do his best, not to guarantee an outcome. The clinician who follows the standard of care has done what the law asks. Medicine discharges that obligation of means and nothing more, and the result is owed by the State, which funds the system, approves the tests and can require what the encounter looks for. Medicine is the means through which the State meets or fails its obligation, and it is medicine as an institution, not the clinician, that has to change.

The doctrinal architecture has been in place since 2000. See [The Instruments Already Exist](/definitions/the-instruments-already-exist/) for the reading of GC14 and the antiretroviral precedent, and [A Litigation Brief](/definitions/a-litigation-brief/) for the standing, justiciability, and remedies analysis a jurist would need to develop the claim.
